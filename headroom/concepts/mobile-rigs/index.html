Mobile Rigs

The DJ setup you carry to a room that doesn't have one. Rigs come in sizes, and the size you pick is a trade between what you can lift and how much air you need to move.

A club rig is a fixed thing you show up to. A mobile rig is a thing you show up with, which makes it a completely different design problem: every part has to justify its weight, its power draw, and the space it takes in a car. The useful way to think about mobile rigs is not by brand but by size — four of them, each one a coherent answer to "how big is the room and how far am I carrying this?"

Below is a working example of each. The gear is what one working setup actually uses; the reasoning is the part worth stealing.

Tiny: laptop only #

The most portable rig is the one already in your bag. The single hurdle is that DJing needs two independent audio outputs — one for the room and one for your headphones — and a laptop ships with one. Everything else about this rig is downstream of solving that.

Do not let the size fool you. A laptop rig can run four decks, and it can route any sound the computer can make: a WhatsApp voice memo from the birthday girl, a YouTube video, a Spotify track somebody insists on. That flexibility is genuinely hard to get on hardware.

  • A small multi-output audio interface. Four channels is plenty. Send the cue to the interface's headphone jack (which gets you a real, physical volume knob instead of a keyboard shortcut) and the mains out of the L/R quarter-inch outputs.
  • Every adapter, every time. You are almost certainly plugging into someone else's speakers, and you will not find out which socket they have until you are standing in front of it. Carry quarter-inch TRS to XLR, TRS to RCA, and TRS to eighth-inch. Three adapters weigh nothing and each one is the difference between playing and not playing.

An adapter changes the shape of a connector, not the electrical nature of the signal. Going from a balanced TRS output into an unbalanced RCA input still works, but you have given up the noise rejection that balanced wiring buys you — so keep that cable run short and expect it to be the first thing that hums.

Small: a controller and a laptop #

Everything here fits in a couple of bags, and unlike the tiny rig it looks like DJing. That matters more than it should: a person hunched over a laptop reads as someone checking email, and a room treats the two differently. You still get four decks (two on the controller, two on the laptop) and you can still route any source you like through the controller's built-in interface.

  • A bus-powered controller. The whole unit runs off a single USB-C cable and brings its own audio interface, which means it also solves the two-outputs problem from the tiny rig. One cable, no power brick, no separate interface.
  • A portable power station. A laptop pulls roughly 75 watts, so a 300 watt-hour battery buys you about four hours (300 ÷ 75 = 4), which covers most parties with room to spare. Watt-hours are the number to shop on, not watts: watts is how fast the tank empties, watt-hours is how big the tank is.
  • Two battery-powered speakers, on stands. Two, not one: a single box collapses your mix to mono and throws away the width every producer put into the record. Battery-powered, because a speaker that needs a wall is a speaker that has to live near a wall — and the whole point of this rig is that it can go where the people are.
  • A table at the right height, and a laptop stand. This is the least glamorous line item and the one people regret skipping. You are going to stand at this thing for four hours. A folding keyboard table adjusts to your height instead of the other way around, and a stand puts the laptop screen up where your eyes already are rather than at the bottom of your neck's range.
  • A clamp-on laptop shelf. Bolts to the table and lifts the laptop above the controller, so the screen and the jog wheels are not competing for the same square foot.

Medium: hardware only #

This is where the laptop goes home. An all-in-one system puts the players, the mixer, and the screens in one box that boots in twenty seconds and has never once shown you a software update notification at 11 p.m. A rig this size comfortably runs a block party for around 150 people.

  • An all-in-one system. Big enough to be genuinely powerful — two decks, a real four-band mixer, a screen you can actually read — and small enough that one person can carry it. That balance is the entire reason this size of rig exists.
  • Folding legs instead of a table. A T-stand workstation collapses to roughly the size of a speaker stand, which means the thing that becomes your table travels like a pole. That is a much better deal than it sounds when you are on your third trip to the car.
  • The smallest flight case that fits. Shop for the low-profile version of whatever case your gear takes. Cases get heavy fast, and a couple of inches is routinely the difference between the case going in the back of a packed car and the case going in somebody else's car.
  • A wireless transmitter, if you want the speakers to roam. A SKAA transmitter sends audio to the speakers over the air. Feeding two speakers costs a fixed 18 milliseconds of delay, which sounds alarming until you convert it: sound covers about a foot per millisecond, so 18 ms is the same lateness as standing twenty feet further back in the room. Your headphones are still wired, so you always have an on-time reference to beatmatch against.
  • Battery speakers on stands, again. Self-powered speakers are what let the room change shape around you. More people arrive, you scoot the whole front line back. It gets late and the crowd thins, you pick up a stand in each hand and walk the dance floor in tighter. Try that with a mains cable.
  • A third small speaker as a booth monitor, if there's room in the car. Facing you, not the crowd, so you are mixing against what you can hear rather than what the room is doing twenty feet away.

Large: generator territory #

Past a certain size the sound system grows a subwoofer, and a subwoofer changes the power arithmetic completely: low frequencies need to move a lot of air, moving air takes real current, and no battery you want to carry will supply it all night. So there is a generator somewhere in this rig, and the rest of the design follows from that fact.

  • This is also the point to stop pairing prosumer battery boxes with a sub and move to proper powered PA cabinets. Consumer speakers and a real subwoofer rarely agree on where the crossover should sit, and you can hear the argument.
  • Play on whatever you like — an all-in-one, standalone players, a laptop. At this size the players are the least of your problems.
  • A real folding table, with flight cases stacked underneath to bring the gear up to a height you can work at without hunching.

Never plug your players straight into the generator. Run the generator into a battery, and your gear off the battery. Generators are slow to respond to a sudden demand for current — the engine has to physically speed up — so a kick drum hitting a subwoofer makes the voltage dip for a moment. Your speakers shrug that off. Your player reboots, and then you are standing in silence for two minutes in front of everyone. The battery sits in between as a buffer that can deliver instantly and is being topped up continuously, so the sag never reaches the equipment that cares about it.

What carries across every size #

  • Two outputs, always. Whatever the rig, something in it has to give you a main mix and a separate cue. That requirement never goes away, it just gets solved by a different box each time.
  • Two speakers, always. Stereo is not a luxury at small sizes. It is most of what makes a mix sound like a record instead of a phone.
  • Ergonomics are equipment. Height, reach, and screen position decide how well you play in hour three. Budget for them like you budget for a mixer.
  • Know your watt-hours. Add up what each device draws, divide the battery's watt-hours by that total, and you have your set length in hours before you arrive rather than after you stop.
  • The chain doesn't care that it's portable. Gain staging and signal flow work exactly the same on a folding table as in a club.
