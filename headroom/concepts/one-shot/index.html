One-Shot

A sample that plays through once when you trigger it and then stops on its own, instead of repeating like a loop.

Press the pad, the sound happens, the sound ends. That is the whole concept. A one-shot has a beginning and an end baked into the file, and the player's only job is to get out of the way until it is over. An air horn, a vocal stab, a single clap, a riser: all one-shots. Contrast that with a loop, which has a start point and an end point that hand back to each other forever until you intervene.

Think of it as the difference between a doorbell and a smoke alarm. One says its piece and stops. The other repeats until somebody deals with it.

Why it matters for DJs #

A one-shot is the cheapest live decision available to you. It costs one finger and about half a second, it does not require you to line up two tracks, and it cannot run away from you the way a loop can. That makes it the tool DJs reach for when a transition needs a seam covered, a drop needs an exclamation mark, or a flat stretch needs a sign of life from the booth.

It works because of how hearing is built. Your auditory system is far more sensitive to onsets (the sudden arrival of a new sound) than to anything steady. That is a survival feature: a constant hum is furniture, a sharp new noise might be a problem. So a short, transient-heavy stab cuts through a dense mix at a volume that seems impossibly low on the meters. See transient for what is actually happening in that first few milliseconds.

Which is also why one-shots are so easy to overuse. The thing that makes them land (novelty) is destroyed by repetition. An air horn is thrilling the first time, funny the second, and a personality trait by the fifth.

Classic one-shots you've definitely heard #

  • The orchestra hit. That stabby BRAAAM of a full orchestra playing one chord, from the Fairlight CMI sampler's factory library (the preset was literally called ORCH5, widely traced to a chord in Stravinsky's The Firebird). Afrika Bambaataa's "Planet Rock" put it on the map in 1982 and it never really left, which is why one Australian sampling machine is responsible for the sound of an entire decade.
  • "Woo! Yeah!" Two syllables lifted from Lyn Collins' 1972 track "Think (About It)", produced by James Brown. Rob Base & DJ E-Z Rock's "It Takes Two" made them the default hype sample, and they have been punctuating records for forty years.
  • The air horn. Straight out of Jamaican sound system culture, where a horn or siren signalled a rewind. It travelled through dancehall into jungle, drum and bass, hip-hop, and eventually every festival main stage on earth.
  • The TR-808 kit. The Roland TR-808's kick, clap, cowbell, and snare are not samples of anything (the machine synthesized them from analog circuits), but they escaped the box long ago and now live as one-shots in every producer's folder. That long, tuned 808 kick is the reason club subwoofers exist in their current form.
  • The reverse cymbal. A crash played backwards, which swells instead of hitting. Free rising tension, no beatmatching required.
  • The vinyl rewind / record scratch. Originally a real physical act, now a file that a million people have pressed to signal "hold on, what?"

Worth knowing what is not a one-shot: the Amen break, the most sampled six seconds in recorded music, is a loop. It is a drum pattern meant to repeat. The distinction is not academic, because the two live on different pads and behave differently the moment you press them.

How your gear handles them #

Most sampler sections (Rekordbox, Serato, Traktor, and the sampler built into battle mixers) let you choose how a pad behaves. The names vary, the behaviours do not:

  • One-shot / trigger. Press and release, the sample plays to the end regardless. Press again mid-playback and it restarts from the top (a retrigger), which is how the machine-gun stutter effect is done.
  • Gate / hold. Sound lasts exactly as long as your finger does. Useful for chopping a long sample into stabs by hand.
  • Loop. Repeats until you press it again. Correct for drum breaks, wrong for air horns unless you are trying to clear a room.

The other setting that matters is quantize. With it on, the player waits and fires your sample on the next beat (or bar, or half beat) of the beat grid, so a sloppy finger still lands in time. With it off, the sample fires the instant you press, which is the only way to get anything deliberately off the grid. Quantize on is the safer default; quantize off is where the feel lives, once your timing has earned it.

Watch your levels. Sampler one-shots are usually mastered loud and often bypass your channel faders entirely, arriving straight at the master bus. A stab that sounded fine in headphones can be the loudest thing of the night through the PA, and it is arriving at exactly the moment everything else is already peaking. Set the sampler's level during soundcheck, not during the drop, and see gain staging for why the master is not the place to fix it.

Using them well #

  • Place them at phrase boundaries. A stab landing on the first beat of a new 16-bar phrase reads as intentional. The same sound four beats later reads as an accident.
  • Use them to hide seams. A one-shot over the moment two tracks swap covers a multitude of sins, because the ear's attention goes to the new onset and away from whatever you were doing underneath.
  • Keep the folder small. Five one-shots you know cold beat fifty you have to hunt through. Hunting costs the attention you need for the room.
  • Check the tail. Some one-shots have reverb tails that hang for two seconds. That tail is part of the sample and it will still be ringing when the next thing arrives.
