Standalone Player

A single deck with its own computer inside. It plays from a USB stick with no laptop attached, but it has no mixer, so it needs one sitting next to it.

This is the CDJ, and the club rig it defines: two or four players in a row, a mixer in the middle, nothing else. Each player is a complete little computer that reads your files, draws the waveform, does the time-stretching, and sends one channel of audio out the back. What it deliberately does not do is mix. Combining those channels, EQing them, and sending the result to the PA is the mixer's job, and the mixer is a separate box you buy separately.

That separation is the whole design. A club can replace a dead player at 2am without touching the mixer, add a fifth deck for a b2b set, or run Pioneer players into a Rane mixer because the DJ asked. Modular gear survives contact with a venue.

Three formats, one question: where does the computer live, and does the box also mix? A controller has no computer and needs your laptop. An all-in-one has the computer and the mixer in one chassis. A standalone player has the computer but no mixer — it's an all-in-one that was taken apart on purpose.

About the name

"CDJ" stands for Compact Disc Jockey, which tells you how old the format is. The disc slot has been vestigial for years and the flagship CDJ-3000 dropped it entirely — everything now arrives on a USB stick, an SD card, or over the network. The name stuck anyway, the way people still "dial" phone numbers. DJs say "CDJ" the way they say "Kleenex," so "standalone player" is the term that also covers Denon and everyone else.

Examples

  • AlphaTheta (Pioneer DJ) CDJ-3000 — the current club standard, and the one most rider requests name by number.
  • CDJ-2000NXS2 — the previous generation, still installed in a great many booths.
  • XDJ-1000MK2 and XDJ-700 — cheaper players in the same family, USB only, aimed at bars and home setups.
  • Denon DJ SC6000 and SC6000M — the main competition, with a bay for an internal SSD and (on the M) a motorized platter that actually spins.
  • Turntables are standalone in the plain sense, but they're not players in this sense — a record needs no computer, which is a different conversation entirely.

Why it matters in the booth

This is the format you will meet in a club whether or not you own one, so it's the one worth learning on. The controls are laid out the way every other DJ expects them, the failure of one deck doesn't take the night with it, and you plug in by handing over a USB stick.

Modern players also link to each other over Ethernet (Pro DJ Link on Pioneer, StagelinQ on Denon), which lets one USB stick feed every deck, shares the beat grid so quantized effects land on the beat, and keeps everyone's tempo displays honest during a b2b. The tradeoff is cost and bulk: four players plus a mixer plus cables is several thousand dollars and a car boot full of gear, which is exactly the problem the all-in-one was invented to solve.

Because the player and the mixer are separate devices, every one of those connections is a place for levels to go wrong. Each deck arrives at the mixer at line level over its own cable, and the mixer's trim is where you match them. That's gain staging with more stages than a controller ever gives you.
